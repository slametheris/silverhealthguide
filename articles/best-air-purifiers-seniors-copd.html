Frequently Asked Questions

Do air purifiers help seniors with COPD?

Yes — HEPA air purifiers remove airborne particles including dust, pet dander, mold spores, and fine particulate matter (PM2.5) that trigger COPD exacerbations. Multiple studies show that HEPA filtration in the bedroom reduces nighttime symptoms and improves sleep quality in COPD patients. Look for True HEPA filtration (removes 99.97% of particles 0.3 microns and larger) combined with activated carbon for gas and odor removal.

What size air purifier do I need for a bedroom?

Air purifier capacity is measured in CADR (Clean Air Delivery Rate) and ACH (Air Changes per Hour). For a 200-square-foot bedroom, look for a CADR of at least 130 and a unit rated for at least 300 square feet. For a larger living room (400 sq ft), look for CADR 200+ and a unit rated for 500+ square feet. A unit that's too small for the room will not adequately clean the air.

Are air purifiers safe for seniors with oxygen therapy?

Yes — HEPA air purifiers are safe to use alongside home oxygen therapy. Avoid ionizing air purifiers and ozone generators, which produce ozone that can irritate airways — particularly dangerous for COPD and asthma patients. Stick to True HEPA + activated carbon filtration. Always consult your pulmonologist if you have concerns about your specific situation.

How often should seniors replace air purifier filters?

HEPA filters typically need replacement every 6–12 months depending on usage and air quality. Activated carbon filters every 3–6 months. Running the purifier continuously accelerates filter wear. Many units have filter replacement indicators. Using a purifier with a heavily loaded filter provides little benefit and can reduce airflow.

Does Medicare cover air purifiers for seniors with COPD?

Original Medicare does not cover air purifiers as durable medical equipment. Some Medicare Advantage plans include home health or wellness benefits that may contribute toward air quality equipment — call your plan and ask specifically. HEPA vacuums and allergen-reducing bedding may be covered under some plans. Check your plan's extra benefits before purchasing.

Seniors spend approximately 90% of their time indoors — and indoor air quality is often significantly more polluted than outdoor air. For seniors with COPD, asthma, allergies, or immune vulnerabilities, a quality air purifier directly reduces symptom burden, improves sleep quality, and reduces respiratory exacerbation risk. Here is what the evidence shows and what to look for.

Why Indoor Air Quality Matters More After 60

Indoor air contains dust mites, pet dander, mold spores, volatile organic compounds (VOCs) from furniture and cleaning products, cooking particulates, and outdoor pollutants that infiltrate through ventilation. Seniors are particularly affected for several reasons: immune function declines with age, making respiratory infections more serious; COPD and asthma are more prevalent; and the time spent indoors is greater. A 2019 study found that HEPA air purification in the bedroom reduced COPD exacerbations by 23% in a 12-month period.

What to Look For — Key Specifications

True HEPA Filtration — Non-Negotiable

True HEPA filters remove 99.97% of airborne particles 0.3 microns or larger — capturing dust mites, pet dander, mold spores, pollen, and fine particulate matter (PM2.5). "HEPA-type" or "HEPA-like" filters perform significantly worse. Look specifically for "True HEPA" or "Medical-grade HEPA" on the label.

Activated Carbon Layer

HEPA alone does not remove gases, odors, or VOCs. Activated carbon filtration captures cooking odors, cleaning product fumes, cigarette smoke residue, and off-gassing from furniture and flooring. For seniors with chemical sensitivities or living in recently renovated spaces, activated carbon is especially important.

CADR Rating Matched to Room Size

CADR (Clean Air Delivery Rate) measures how quickly the purifier cleans the air in a given space. As a rule: CADR should be at least 2/3 of your room's square footage. A 300 sq ft bedroom needs CADR 200+. Running a small purifier in a large room provides minimal benefit.

Avoid Ozone-Generating Technology

Ionizers, ozone generators, and some UV-C purifiers produce ozone as a byproduct — a lung irritant that is particularly dangerous for COPD and asthma patients. Stick to HEPA + activated carbon filtration. If a unit claims to "purify air with ions" or "eliminate bacteria with ozone," avoid it for seniors with respiratory conditions.

Where to Place for Maximum Benefit

Priority location: bedroom. Seniors spend 7–9 hours in the bedroom — more continuous exposure to clean air during sleep produces the most meaningful health benefit. Place the purifier within 6 feet of the bed, not in a corner where airflow is restricted.

Secondary: living room or main sitting area. If budget allows a second unit, place it where you spend the most daytime hours.

Not recommended: Closets, narrow alcoves, or directly against walls — restrict airflow and reduce effectiveness by 30–40%.

Filter Maintenance for Seniors

A clogged filter is nearly useless and can reduce airflow to the point where the purifier is actively blowing unfiltered air. Check the filter indicator light monthly. Vacuum the pre-filter (outer filter) every 2–4 weeks to extend HEPA filter life. Replace HEPA filter every 6–12 months. Many premium purifiers have replacement subscription services that simplify this.
