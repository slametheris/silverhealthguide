Peripheral Neuropathy in Seniors: Causes, Symptoms, and Treatment Options

RO
By Dr. Rachel Okonkwo, MD, Neurology
🕐 9 min read
✅ Expert reviewed

Frequently Asked Questions

What is peripheral neuropathy?

Peripheral neuropathy is damage to the peripheral nerves — the nerves outside the brain and spinal cord that carry signals to and from the limbs, organs, and skin. It causes numbness, tingling, burning, or pain typically starting in the feet and hands, and in some cases muscle weakness. Peripheral neuropathy is not a single disease but a symptom of many underlying conditions, the most common of which is diabetes.

What causes peripheral neuropathy in seniors?

The most common causes in seniors: diabetic neuropathy (the leading cause — affects 50% of people with diabetes over time), vitamin B12 deficiency (common in seniors, especially those on metformin), alcohol-related neuropathy, chemotherapy-induced neuropathy, hypothyroidism, kidney disease, autoimmune conditions (lupus, rheumatoid arthritis), and idiopathic (no identifiable cause — accounts for 30% of cases). Identifying the cause is critical because treating the underlying condition can halt or reverse neuropathy progression.

How is peripheral neuropathy diagnosed?

Diagnosis typically includes: physical examination (pin-prick sensation, vibration testing, reflexes, monofilament testing for diabetic neuropathy), nerve conduction studies (NCS) and electromyography (EMG) — the definitive test for type and severity, blood work to identify treatable causes (B12, thyroid, glucose, kidney function, autoimmune markers), and in some cases nerve biopsy or genetic testing for hereditary causes. Medicare Part B covers diagnostic testing when ordered by a physician.

Can peripheral neuropathy be reversed?

Reversal depends on the cause. Diabetic neuropathy: aggressive blood sugar control slows progression and in early stages may allow some improvement; established neuropathy is rarely fully reversed. B12 deficiency neuropathy: B12 supplementation or injections often significantly improves or fully reverses symptoms, especially if caught early. Chemotherapy-induced neuropathy: often partially improves after chemotherapy ends. Idiopathic neuropathy: management-focused rather than reversal-oriented. Early identification and treatment of the cause gives the best chance of halting progression.

Does Medicare cover neuropathy treatment?

Medicare Part B covers diagnostic testing (nerve conduction studies, EMG), physician consultations with neurologists, physical therapy for neuropathy-related balance and gait problems, and pain management consultations. Medicare Part D covers prescription medications for neuropathic pain (gabapentin, pregabalin, duloxetine). Foot care: Medicare covers routine foot care for beneficiaries with diabetic peripheral neuropathy — inspect feet at medical visits, diabetic shoe coverage annually.

Peripheral neuropathy affects approximately 20 million Americans — and prevalence rises sharply with age and diabetes duration. Many seniors attribute the tingling, numbness, and burning in their feet to "just getting old" — missing a treatable underlying cause or delaying pain management that can dramatically improve quality of life. Here is what seniors and their families need to know.

Recognizing the Symptoms

Peripheral neuropathy typically begins in the longest nerves first — the feet and lower legs, then the hands. Symptoms range from sensory (feeling-based) to motor (movement-based):

Sensory symptoms: Numbness or reduced sensation in feet or hands, tingling ("pins and needles"), burning pain (often worse at night), sharp stabbing pains, extreme sensitivity to touch (even the weight of bedsheets can be painful), feeling of walking on cotton or sand when barefoot.

Motor symptoms: Muscle weakness in feet or hands, difficulty with fine motor tasks (buttoning clothing, picking up small objects), foot drop (catching the toe when walking), frequent unexplained falls due to reduced foot sensation and proprioception.

Autonomic symptoms (when autonomic nerves are affected): Blood pressure drops when standing (orthostatic hypotension), abnormal sweating, digestive problems (nausea, diarrhea, constipation), urinary retention.

Identifying and Treating the Cause

Diabetic Neuropathy — Most Common

Affects 50% of people with long-term diabetes. Caused by hyperglycemia-induced damage to nerve blood vessels. Prevention and management: the most important intervention is tight blood sugar control — each percentage point reduction in A1c significantly reduces neuropathy progression risk. Once established, neuropathy-specific treatments address symptoms rather than cause. Annual diabetic foot exams with monofilament testing are free under Medicare and essential for detecting severity changes.

Vitamin B12 Deficiency — Most Treatable

Particularly important: metformin (the most-prescribed diabetes medication) reduces B12 absorption in 10–30% of long-term users. Seniors on metformin should have B12 levels tested annually. B12 deficiency neuropathy responds well to supplementation or injections — early treatment can fully reverse symptoms. A serum B12 below 300 pg/mL in a symptomatic patient warrants treatment trial regardless of strict "normal" range definitions.

Alcohol-Related Neuropathy

Chronic heavy alcohol use causes both direct nerve toxicity and nutritional deficiencies (B1/thiamine, B12, folate). Reducing alcohol intake and correcting nutritional deficiencies stabilizes symptoms and allows partial recovery.

Pain Management

When addressing the underlying cause is insufficient, evidence-based pain management options include:

First-line medications: Gabapentin (Neurontin) and pregabalin (Lyrica) are FDA-approved for neuropathic pain — they reduce nerve signal abnormalities. Duloxetine (Cymbalta) has FDA approval for diabetic neuropathic pain. These require prescription and carry their own side effect profiles in seniors (dizziness, sedation, edema).

Topical treatments (fewer systemic side effects): Lidocaine patches (Lidoderm) for localized pain. Capsaicin cream (must be applied with gloves — causes burning initially that diminishes with regular use). Topical diclofenac for musculoskeletal components of foot pain.

Non-pharmacological: Physical therapy improves balance and reduces fall risk from neuropathy-related proprioception loss. TENS (transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation) provides symptom relief for some patients. Biofeedback for pain management. Regular low-impact exercise (swimming, cycling) maintains nerve function.

Fall Risk — Critical Safety Consideration

Peripheral neuropathy dramatically increases fall risk — reduced foot sensation removes the proprioceptive feedback that normally prevents stumbling. Steps to mitigate: wear properly fitting shoes with good ankle support at all times (including indoors — never barefoot), install adequate nightlighting on paths to bathroom, use a cane or walker if balance is significantly affected, and participate in a fall prevention exercise program (balance training specifically addresses the proprioception deficits neuropathy causes).

  • Get a serum B12 test — deficiency is the most reversible cause of neuropathy
  • If on metformin for diabetes, request annual B12 monitoring
  • Tight blood sugar control is the most important intervention for diabetic neuropathy
  • Report neuropathy symptoms to your doctor — they are not inevitable with aging
  • Ask for a neurologist referral if symptoms are progressing or cause has not been identified
  • Never walk barefoot — foot injuries may go unnoticed with reduced sensation
