Loss of Appetite in Seniors: Causes, When to Worry, and What Helps

MC
By Margaret Chen, PharmD
🕐 7 min read
✅ Expert reviewed

Frequently Asked Questions

Is loss of appetite normal with aging?

Appetite tends to decline somewhat with age because of slower digestion, changes in taste and smell, and lower energy needs. But a persistent, noticeable drop in eating — or unintended weight loss — is not something to simply accept. It can signal depression, dental problems, medication side effects, or an underlying illness that is treatable.

When is weight loss a reason to see a doctor?

Unintentional weight loss of about 5% of body weight over 6 to 12 months (roughly 7 to 8 pounds for a 150-pound person) is a commonly used threshold for medical evaluation. Faster loss, or loss together with weakness, difficulty swallowing, or persistent pain, warrants prompt attention.

Which medications can reduce appetite?

Many can, including some antibiotics, certain heart and blood pressure drugs, some antidepressants, opioid pain relievers, and metformin. Never stop a medication on your own — ask your doctor or pharmacist whether a medicine could be affecting your appetite and whether alternatives exist.

How can I get more nutrition if I can only eat small amounts?

Eat small meals and snacks every two to three hours, put protein first, add healthy calories to foods you already eat (olive oil, nut butter, whole milk, cheese), and use strong flavors. A protein-rich shake can help fill gaps if your doctor or dietitian agrees.

Does Medicare cover nutrition counseling?

Original Medicare covers medical nutrition therapy with a registered dietitian for people with diabetes, chronic kidney disease (not on dialysis), or after a kidney transplant, with a doctor's referral. It does not generally cover nutrition counseling for other conditions, but some Medicare Advantage plans offer extra nutrition or meal benefits.

When appetite fades, strength, immunity, and independence can fade with it. Because older adults have less nutritional reserve, even a few weeks of eating too little can lead to muscle loss and a higher risk of falls and infections. The first step is finding out why.

Common Causes

  • Medications: a frequent and fixable cause — review your list with a pharmacist
  • Dental or swallowing problems: painful teeth, ill-fitting dentures, or difficulty swallowing
  • Depression, grief, or loneliness: eating alone is a strong appetite suppressor
  • Changes in taste and smell: food may seem bland or unpleasant
  • Medical conditions: digestive disorders, thyroid problems, infections, heart or kidney disease, and cancer
  • Early cognitive decline: forgetting to eat or no longer recognizing hunger

Warning Signs That Need Medical Attention

  • Unintended weight loss of about 5% over 6–12 months, or any rapid loss
  • Pain or difficulty when swallowing, or frequent choking or coughing with meals
  • Persistent nausea, vomiting, or abdominal pain
  • Clothes and dentures becoming loose
  • Low mood, withdrawal, or confusion alongside the eating change

Practical Ways to Eat Better

  1. Small, frequent meals. Five or six small meals can be easier than three large ones.
  2. Protein first. Eggs, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, fish, and beans protect muscle. See our protein guide.
  3. Fortify what you already eat. Add olive oil, nut butter, grated cheese, or powdered milk to soups, oatmeal, and mashed potatoes.
  4. Boost flavor. Herbs, lemon, spices, and umami-rich ingredients can make food more appealing.
  5. Eat with others. Shared meals, a senior center lunch program, or Meals on Wheels through your Area Agency on Aging can help.
  6. Drink between meals rather than before, so liquids don't fill you up.
  • Don't write off a poor appetite as 'just aging' — ask why
  • Have your medications and your teeth checked as possible causes
  • Weigh yourself regularly and note unintended loss
  • Eat small, protein-rich meals and snacks throughout the day
  • Seek meal and social programs through your local Area Agency on Aging (eldercare.acl.gov)
