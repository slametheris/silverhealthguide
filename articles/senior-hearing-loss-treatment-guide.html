Hearing Loss in Seniors: Treatment Options from OTC Aids to Cochlear Implants

SM
By Dr. Sandra Mills, RN
🕐 9 min read
✅ Expert reviewed

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the treatment options for hearing loss in seniors?

Treatment options range from least to most invasive: OTC hearing aids (for mild-to-moderate loss, no prescription needed, $200–$1,500), prescription hearing aids from an audiologist ($3,000–$7,000 per pair, better customization and follow-up), bone-anchored hearing devices for specific types of hearing loss, cochlear implants for severe-to-profound loss (Medicare-covered surgery), and assistive listening devices for situational use.

What is the difference between OTC and prescription hearing aids?

OTC hearing aids became available without prescription in 2022 for adults with mild-to-moderate hearing loss. They cost $200–$1,500 vs $3,000–$7,000 for prescription aids. The trade-off: OTC aids are self-fitted, without professional hearing assessment, customization, or follow-up care. Prescription aids are programmed to your specific audiogram by an audiologist, with ongoing adjustments. For moderate-to-severe loss or complex hearing situations, prescription aids typically provide better outcomes.

Does Medicare cover hearing aids?

Original Medicare (Parts A and B) does not cover hearing aids or routine hearing exams. Medicare Advantage plans often include hearing benefits with annual allowances ($500–$2,500) toward hearing aids. Some Advantage plans partner with providers like TruHearing or HearUSA. During AEP, compare plans specifically on hearing benefits if you need hearing aids. Medicaid covers hearing aids for dual-eligible seniors in many states.

Does Medicare cover cochlear implants?

Yes — Medicare Part B covers cochlear implant surgery and the external processor as medically necessary durable medical equipment for qualifying patients with severe-to-profound sensorineural hearing loss who do not benefit sufficiently from conventional hearing aids. Coverage includes the surgery, device, initial programming, and follow-up care. Pre-authorization is typically required.

Should seniors try OTC hearing aids first?

For mild-to-moderate hearing loss, OTC hearing aids are a reasonable and cost-effective starting point — particularly for seniors who want to try amplification without the upfront cost of prescription aids. However, a baseline hearing evaluation by an audiologist before purchasing OTC aids is strongly recommended — it identifies the type and degree of hearing loss, ruling out medically treatable causes and ensuring OTC aids are appropriate for your specific loss.

Hearing loss affects two in three Americans over 70 — and treating it is now recognized as the single most impactful action for preventing dementia. The Lancet Commission on Dementia identified untreated hearing loss as the leading modifiable dementia risk factor, with a 90% higher dementia risk in those with severe untreated loss. OTC Hearing Aids — The New Access Point

Since 2022, FDA-approved OTC hearing aids are available at pharmacies, big-box stores, and online without a prescription for adults with mild to moderate hearing loss. Notable options:

  • Jabra Enhance: Strong reputation for sound quality, available at Costco and Jabra.com, $800–$1,800
  • Sony CRE: Backed by Sony's audio engineering, approximately $1,300 per pair
  • Lexie Powered by Bose: $849–$999, excellent app-based control
  • Eargo: Invisible-in-canal design, $1,000–$1,650

OTC aids are appropriate for those who: have already had their hearing evaluated and know they have mild-to-moderate loss, are comfortable with app-based self-fitting, and prefer to trial hearing aids at lower cost before committing to prescription aids.

Prescription Hearing Aids — Higher Performance

Prescription hearing aids are programmed by an audiologist to your specific audiogram — tuned to your exact hearing loss pattern, not a general average. Benefits: more precise fitting, better speech understanding in noise, ongoing professional follow-up and adjustments as your hearing changes, and better outcomes for moderate-to-severe loss. Cost: $3,000–$7,000 per pair.

Prescription aids available at Costco Hearing Centers start around $1,500 — significantly lower than traditional audiology practices while still including professional fitting and follow-up. Costco is one of the highest-volume hearing aid providers in the US and consistently earns high satisfaction ratings.

Medicare Advantage Hearing Benefits

Many Medicare Advantage plans include annual hearing allowances. Coverage varies significantly: $500 to $2,500 annual allowance toward hearing aids. Some plans partner with TruHearing, HearUSA, or other networks, which typically offer prescription-grade aids at $400–$1,400 per pair through the network. During AEP, compare plans specifically on hearing benefits if you need hearing aids — this benefit can represent thousands of dollars in savings.

Cochlear Implants — For Severe Loss

Cochlear implants surgically bypass damaged hair cells and directly stimulate the auditory nerve. Appropriate for adults with severe-to-profound sensorineural hearing loss who don't benefit adequately from hearing aids. Medicare Part B covers cochlear implant surgery, the internal device, and the external processor as medically necessary durable medical equipment. Your 20% coinsurance applies; with Medigap Plan G, surgery and device costs are covered after the annual Part B deductible. Pre-authorization from your Medicare Advantage plan or Part B is required.

Assistive Listening Devices

For situational hearing challenges rather than all-day hearing loss: TV amplifiers (hearing directly through headphones or neck loops), phone amplifiers, captioned telephones (CapTel — free through state telecommunications programs in most states), alerting devices (flashing lights for doorbells and alarms), and loop systems for theaters and public spaces. These devices complement rather than replace hearing aids for many seniors.
