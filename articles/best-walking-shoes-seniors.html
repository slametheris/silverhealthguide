Frequently Asked Questions

What should seniors look for in walking shoes?

The most important features for seniors are: non-slip rubber outsoles with deep tread (fall prevention), wide toe box (accommodates bunions, hammer toes, and toe deformities common in seniors), adequate arch support and cushioning, firm heel counter (stability), removable insoles (for custom orthotics), and lightweight construction. Avoid smooth leather soles and narrow pointed toe boxes.

What is the best walking shoe for seniors with balance problems?

For seniors with balance problems, look for shoes with wider heel bases (more stable platform), firm heel counters, low heels close to the ground, non-slip soles, and a snug lace or strap closure. Avoid slip-on shoes without heel backing, worn soles, and any heel elevation. New Balance, Brooks, and ASICS offer models specifically designed for stability.

Are slip-on shoes safe for seniors?

Slip-on shoes without heel backing are a fall risk for seniors — they require toe gripping to keep them on, altering gait and reducing stability. If slip-ons are needed for ease of dressing, choose those with a firm back and heel strap, or use adaptive lacing systems that convert lace-up shoes to slip-ons while maintaining a secure fit. Velcro closures are an excellent alternative for seniors who struggle with laces.

What shoes are best for seniors with diabetes?

Seniors with diabetes need extra-depth diabetic shoes with seamless interiors (prevent pressure points that can cause ulcers), wide toe boxes (prevent compression of toe deformities), soft non-abrasive materials, and removable insoles for custom diabetic insoles. Medicare Part B covers therapeutic shoes for Medicare beneficiaries with diabetes — one pair per year with three pairs of insoles.

Does Medicare cover orthopedic shoes for seniors?

Medicare Part B covers therapeutic footwear for Medicare beneficiaries with diabetic peripheral neuropathy — one pair of extra-depth shoes and three pairs of insoles per calendar year. Non-diabetic orthopedic or orthotic needs are generally not covered by Medicare. Custom orthotics prescribed by a doctor may be partially covered as durable medical equipment when medically necessary.

Falls are the leading cause of injury death in adults over 65 — and inappropriate footwear is a contributing factor in up to 50% of senior falls. The right walking shoe does more than prevent blisters: it provides the stability, grip, and support that protect against falls, reduce joint pain, and make daily walking more comfortable and sustainable. As a physical therapist, footwear is one of my first assessments with every senior patient.

📌 Most Important Rule

Replace your walking shoes every 300–400 miles of use, or every 12 months — whichever comes first. The cushioning and stability features of athletic shoes degrade significantly with wear, long before the exterior shows obvious damage. Worn-out shoes are a fall risk.

The 7 Features Every Senior Walking Shoe Must Have

1. Non-Slip Rubber Outsole with Aggressive Tread

The single most important fall-prevention feature. Deep rubber tread provides grip on wet floors, uneven pavement, and slick surfaces. Avoid smooth leather soles, worn rubber, and any outsole that shows visible wear patterns. Test grip by twisting the shoe — adequate tread should resist the twist.

2. Wide Toe Box

Most senior feet have widened, developed bunions, or have hammer toes from decades of wear. A narrow toe box creates pressure points, alters gait, and causes pain. The toes should have room to spread naturally without compression. When fitting shoes, there should be a thumbnail's width of space between the longest toe and the end of the shoe.

3. Firm Heel Counter

The heel counter is the rigid backing at the rear of the shoe. A firm heel counter controls rearfoot motion and provides the stability that prevents ankle rolling — one of the most common fall mechanisms. Press your thumb against the back of the shoe — it should offer firm resistance, not collapse easily.

4. Adequate Cushioning (Not Too Much)

Cushioning absorbs impact and reduces joint stress — important for seniors with arthritis. However, extremely thick cushioning (like maximalist shoes) can reduce proprioception (the sensory feedback that tells your brain where your foot is) and actually increase fall risk. Moderate cushioning in the 10–20mm range is optimal for most seniors.

5. Low Heel Drop

Heel drop is the difference in height between the heel and forefoot. Shoes with low heel drop (4–8mm) keep your foot closer to the ground and promote more natural walking mechanics. High heels — even modest 25–30mm dress shoes — shift the body's center of gravity forward and increase fall risk.

6. Secure Closure

Shoes that fit securely reduce compensatory toe gripping. Lace-up shoes with tied laces provide the most secure fit. Velcro closures are an excellent alternative for seniors who struggle with laces — they allow customized width adjustment and eliminate tripping hazards from loose laces. Avoid unstrapped slip-ons for outdoor use.

7. Removable Insoles

Removable insoles allow you to insert custom orthotics if needed. If you have been prescribed orthotics by a podiatrist or physiatrist, you need shoes with removable insoles — or the orthotics will not fit correctly.

How to Properly Fit Walking Shoes for Seniors

Always try on walking shoes in the afternoon or evening — feet swell throughout the day. Wear the same socks you will walk in. There should be a thumbnail's width between your longest toe and the end of the shoe. The widest part of your foot should align with the widest part of the shoe without feeling squeezed. The heel should feel snug with minimal slipping. Walk in the shoes in the store — not just stand.

  • Replace walking shoes every 12 months or 300–400 miles — worn shoes are a fall risk
  • Shop in the afternoon when feet are at their largest
  • Choose non-slip rubber outsoles with deep tread — check for wear regularly
  • Ensure wide toe box — toes should not be compressed at all
  • Avoid slip-on shoes without heel backing for outdoor use
  • If you have diabetes, ask your podiatrist about Medicare-covered diabetic shoes
