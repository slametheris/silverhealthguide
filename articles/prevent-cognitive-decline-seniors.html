Frequently Asked Questions

Can cognitive decline be prevented?

The 2024 Lancet Commission concluded that up to 40% of dementia cases are preventable or delayable through lifestyle modification. The most impactful modifiable risk factors are untreated hearing loss, high blood pressure, physical inactivity, smoking, depression, social isolation, air pollution, diabetes, obesity, excessive alcohol, and traumatic brain injury. Addressing these factors significantly reduces lifetime dementia risk.

What is the single best thing to do to prevent dementia?

Based on current evidence, treating hearing loss has the largest individual impact on dementia prevention — it is the #1 modifiable risk factor identified by the Lancet Commission. Untreated moderate hearing loss nearly triples dementia risk. After hearing, aerobic exercise has the strongest protective evidence — it grows the hippocampus (memory center) and reduces Alzheimer's risk by an estimated 30–40%.

Does exercise prevent dementia?

Yes — aerobic exercise is the most evidence-backed intervention for brain protection. Studies show that regular aerobic exercise increases brain volume (particularly in the hippocampus), improves memory and executive function, and reduces Alzheimer's risk by 30–45%. Even 30 minutes of brisk walking 5 days per week produces measurable brain benefits within 6 months.

Does learning new skills prevent dementia?

Yes — cognitive engagement through learning new complex skills (not just puzzles) builds cognitive reserve — the brain's resilience against damage. Learning a musical instrument, a new language, or a complex craft has stronger evidence than crossword puzzles alone. The key word is 'new' — activities you already know well provide less benefit than genuinely challenging new learning.

At what age should you start worrying about dementia prevention?

Prevention is most effective when started in midlife (40s–50s), but intervention at any age reduces risk. The Lancet Commission specifically emphasizes that risk factors in your 40s (high blood pressure, obesity, hearing loss) have outsized effects on dementia outcomes in your 70s and 80s. It is never too late to start — meaningful risk reduction occurs from lifestyle changes even after age 70.

The 10 Evidence-Based Strategies

1. Treat Hearing Loss — #1 Modifiable Risk Factor

Untreated hearing loss is the single largest modifiable dementia risk factor identified by the Lancet Commission. Moderate untreated hearing loss nearly triples dementia risk. The mechanism: hearing loss increases cognitive load (brain works harder to process sound), reduces social engagement, and accelerates auditory cortex atrophy. Get a hearing test. If hearing aids are recommended, use them consistently — studies show hearing aid use reduces cognitive decline rates.

2. Aerobic Exercise — Grows Brain Volume

The hippocampus (memory center) actually shrinks with age — but regular aerobic exercise reverses this. A landmark study showed that 1 year of aerobic exercise increased hippocampal volume by 2% in older adults — equivalent to reversing 1–2 years of age-related brain shrinkage. Target: 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity per week. Walking, cycling, and swimming all qualify.

3. Control Blood Pressure — Starting in Your 40s

Midlife hypertension (blood pressure above 130/80 in your 40s and 50s) significantly increases dementia risk decades later by damaging small blood vessels throughout the brain. The SPRINT-MIND trial showed intensive blood pressure control reduced mild cognitive impairment risk by 19%. Blood pressure control is one of the most impactful brain protection strategies — and also protects your heart and kidneys.

4. Prioritize Sleep — Brain Cleans Itself During Sleep

During deep sleep, the brain's glymphatic system activates and flushes out amyloid-beta — the toxic protein that accumulates in Alzheimer's disease. Chronic sleep deprivation (below 6 hours consistently) is associated with significantly higher amyloid accumulation and faster cognitive decline. Seven to 8 hours of quality sleep per night is a direct brain protection strategy.

5. Stay Socially Engaged

Social isolation is a significant independent dementia risk factor — comparable in effect size to physical inactivity. Regular meaningful social interaction provides cognitive stimulation, reduces depression (itself a risk factor), and maintains neural network complexity. Join clubs, volunteer, attend religious services, stay connected with family — the specific activity matters less than the consistent engagement.

6. Manage Depression — Treat It, Don't Accept It

Depression is both a risk factor for and an early symptom of dementia. Untreated depression accelerates neurodegeneration. The good news: treatment works — antidepressants, therapy (especially CBT), and exercise all effectively treat depression in seniors, and treatment may reduce dementia risk. Never accept depression as inevitable aging.

7. Learn Genuinely New, Complex Skills

Cognitive reserve — the brain's resilience against damage — is built by challenging new learning. Learning a musical instrument, a new language, painting, or chess builds more reserve than existing hobbies. Crossword puzzles help if they are challenging; familiar ones do not. The key principle: difficulty and novelty drive the neural growth that builds reserve.

8. Follow the MIND Diet

The MIND diet (Mediterranean-DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay) was specifically designed for brain protection. Its strongest evidence: eating leafy greens at least 6 times per week, berries at least twice weekly, and fish at least once weekly. One observational study found MIND diet adherents had a 53% reduced Alzheimer's risk and a 35% reduction even with moderate adherence.

9. Stop Smoking — Dementia Risk Increases With Every Pack-Year

Smoking approximately doubles dementia risk through vascular damage, oxidative stress, and direct neurotoxicity. Critically, quitting smoking at any age reduces dementia risk — the brain's vasculature begins recovering within months of cessation. Medicare covers tobacco cessation counseling — ask your doctor for a referral.

10. Limit Alcohol to 1 Drink Per Day Maximum

Heavy alcohol use (more than 3–4 drinks daily) causes direct neurotoxicity and is a significant dementia risk factor. Even moderate drinking above 14 drinks per week in later life is associated with accelerated brain shrinkage. One drink per day is the maximum consistent with brain health — and abstention is better for the brain than moderate consumption.
